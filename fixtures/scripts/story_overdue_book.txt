The book that came back

As a grandmother of three, I thought I had seen every excuse for a late book. I'm also the only librarian in Alder Creek, Oregon, a town of 2,300 people, one stoplight, and a library the size of a two-car garage.

Last October, a padded envelope arrived with no return address. The postmark said Portland, which meant nothing to me at the time. Inside was a hardcover novel called The Long Way Home, wrapped in a grocery bag. The checkout card was still in its paper pocket. It had been borrowed on March 9, 1983, and it was due back on March 23, 1983.

Under the book was a stack of cash: $1,554.60. I counted it twice. Our late fee is ten cents a day, and when I did the math on a calculator, the fine came out to exactly that, to the penny. There was a note in shaky blue ink: "Sorry it took so long. Please give the money to whoever needs it most."

My coworker Marisol looked at the cash and said, "Either that's a confession or a ransom."

The checkout card had one name on it: R. Okafor. But when I opened the book, the margins were full of pencil notes in two different handwritings. One was small and neat. The other was big and messy. They were arguing about the story, chapter by chapter. The messy writer signed every note "R." The neat writer signed every note "J."

"The ending is too sad," R. wrote on page 40. "The ending is the point," J. wrote back underneath.

I spent a weekend in our basement with the old card catalog boxes. The library kept every checkout card from before we went digital in 1998. I found that The Long Way Home had been checked out 26 times in 1982, every two weeks, alternating between R. Okafor and J. Lind. They had never written a word to each other anywhere but in that book. The library was their mailbox.

The last note in the book was from J., inside the back cover: "Same time next week? J." There was no reply. After March 1983, R. Okafor never checked out another book.

J. Lind was easy to find. June Lind still lives on Cedar Street, four blocks from the library, and she still comes in every Tuesday. When I showed her the book, she sat down very slowly. She told me she never learned who R. was. She waited at the return desk every Tuesday for a month in 1983, and then she stopped waiting. She married, raised two sons, and lost her husband in 2015.

"I always assumed R. just got bored of me," she said.

I posted on the town's Facebook page asking if anyone remembered an R. Okafor. Three days later, a man named Robert Okafor sent me a message from Portland. He was 19 in 1983. His father lost his job that March, and the family moved to Portland in one weekend. He took the book by accident in a box of his things. By the time he found it, he was too embarrassed to send it back, and too shy to write to someone he only knew as J.

He said he kept the book for 42 years because it was the only thing he had of her. He finally mailed it when his doctor told him to put his affairs in order. He sent the money so the library would not be cheated.

I asked him if he wanted to return it himself.

The following Tuesday, a man in a gray coat walked into the library holding a canvas bag that had seen better decades. June was at the return desk. Neither of them said anything for a long time. Then Robert put the book on the counter and said, "Sorry. It's a little overdue."

I stamped it RETURNED with that day's date. It was the first stamp on that card since 1983.

They had coffee across the street for three hours. Now they read the same book every month, each with their own copy, and they still write in the margins before they trade. June says R. still thinks every ending is too sad.

We used the $1,554.60 to build a reading nook by the front window, with two chairs facing each other. Marisol made the sign. It says "The Long Way Home."

The fine was ten cents a day. It turned out to be the cheapest thing in the whole story.
