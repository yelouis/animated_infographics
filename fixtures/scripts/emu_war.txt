The Great Emu War

In 1932, the farmers of Western Australia were losing a war, and their enemy had feathers.

After the First World War, thousands of returning soldiers were given land to grow wheat. Then the Great Depression hit, wheat prices collapsed, and a drought pushed about 20,000 emus inland, straight onto their farms. The birds trampled crops and flattened fences, letting rabbits in behind them.

The farmers asked the government for help, and the government sent the army. Major G. P. W. Meredith of the Royal Australian Artillery arrived in the Campion district with two soldiers, two Lewis machine guns, and 10,000 rounds of ammunition.

The first attack came on November 2. The soldiers spotted about fifty emus and opened fire. The birds split into small groups and ran out of range. Two days later, the soldiers set an ambush near a dam, where more than a thousand emus were gathering. The gun jammed after killing only twelve.

Newspapers mocked the operation, and the army withdrew within a week. Meredith returned in mid November and kept hunting until December 10. By then his men had fired 9,860 rounds and claimed 986 kills. That is about ten bullets for every bird.

Meredith was impressed by his opponent. He said that if he had a division of soldiers with the bullet-carrying capacity of these birds, it would face any army in the world.

In the end, the emus kept coming. The government gave up on machine guns and paid farmers a bounty instead. In six months of 1934, farmers claimed 57,034 bounties. Today, Western Australia still uses long barrier fences to keep emus out of its wheat fields.
