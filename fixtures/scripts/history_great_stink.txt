The Great Stink

In the summer of 1858, London could not breathe.

The city had grown to more than two million people, and almost all of their waste ended up in the River Thames. Toilets drained into old sewers, the old sewers drained into the river, and the river was also where many Londoners got their drinking water. That June, a heat wave turned the Thames into a slow, brown, steaming soup. Newspapers called it the Great Stink.

The smell reached the new Houses of Parliament, which sat right on the riverbank. Members of Parliament soaked their curtains in chloride of lime to block the stench. Some held handkerchiefs to their faces during debates. There was serious talk of moving the government out of London altogether.

Almost everyone in Victorian Britain believed that disease was carried by bad air. They called it miasma. If something smelled terrible, the thinking went, it was making you sick. So the stink was not just disgusting. It was terrifying.

One man disagreed. Four years earlier, in 1854, cholera had swept through Soho and killed 616 people in a few weeks. A doctor named John Snow went door to door and marked every death on a map. The dots clustered around a single water pump on Broad Street. Snow convinced local officials to remove the pump's handle, and the outbreak faded. His conclusion was that cholera spread through dirty water, not dirty air. Most experts ignored him.

John Snow died on June 16, 1858, in the middle of the Great Stink, before anyone admitted he was right.

Parliament, desperate to escape the smell, finally acted. Within weeks, it gave the job and the money to Joseph Bazalgette, the chief engineer of the Metropolitan Board of Works. Bazalgette already had a plan. Instead of letting sewers pour into the Thames in the middle of the city, he would intercept them. Giant new sewers would run alongside the river, catch the waste before it reached the water, and carry it east, far downstream of where people lived.

The scale was enormous. His team built 82 miles of main intercepting sewers and about 1,100 miles of street sewers. They used 318 million bricks. Along the river, they built embankments that hid the new sewers underground and gave London new roads and gardens on top. Huge steam pumping stations, like Crossness, lifted the sewage so it could keep flowing downhill to the outfalls.

Bazalgette made one decision that still matters. When he worked out how wide each pipe needed to be, he doubled it. The story goes that he said, "We're only going to do this once." London's population would more than triple over the next century, and his pipes kept up.

Here is the twist. Bazalgette and Parliament built the system to get rid of the smell, because they believed the smell was the danger. They were wrong about why people were dying. But by keeping sewage out of the drinking water, the new sewers stopped cholera anyway.

The proof came in 1866. Cholera broke out again, but this time almost all of the deaths were in one part of East London that had not yet been connected to the new system, and whose water company was still drawing from a dirty river. Doctors finally had to accept what John Snow had shown with a map and a pump handle twelve years earlier.

Bazalgette was knighted in 1875 and died in 1891. Much of what he built is still in use today, under streets where millions of people walk without thinking about it.

Even his oversized pipes eventually filled up. In the 2010s, London started digging a new super sewer, a 25-kilometer tunnel deep under the Thames, to catch the overflow that Bazalgette's system could no longer handle.

Snow got a pub named after him on the street where the pump stood. Bazalgette got a monument on the embankment he built. The river got cleaner. And London learned, a little late, that the thing that smelled worst was not the thing that killed.
